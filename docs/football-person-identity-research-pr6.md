## PR6 retained research

### Bobby Layne — QB

1. At Texas he was an elite baseball pitcher with a 35-3 career record, a 28-0 Southwest Conference mark, and two no-hitters in 1946.
2. In the 1946 Cotton Bowl he accounted for every Texas point in a 40-point performance, scoring four touchdowns, kicking four extra points, and throwing two touchdown passes.
3. His friendship with Doak Walker carried into Detroit, where the pair became central to the Lions' championship identity and Walker kicked the deciding extra point after a late Layne-led drive in the 1953 title game.
4. His Texas career was interrupted by service in the U.S. Merchant Marine before he returned to the Longhorns.
5. He formed a close relationship with coach Buddy Parker in Detroit and was eventually traded to Parker's Pittsburgh Steelers in 1958.

Source summary: Texas Athletics and the Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source IDs: `identity-layne-texas-baseball-hof`, `identity-layne-texas-college-hof`, `identity-layne-texas-merchant-marine`, `identity-layne-pro-hof`.

### Drew Brees — QB

1. He led Westlake to a 16-0 Texas 5A state-title season, yet Purdue and Kentucky were the only major programs he recalled seriously recruiting him.
2. His final Chargers game ended with a catastrophic throwing-shoulder dislocation that included a 360-degree labrum tear and rotator-cuff damage.
3. During rehabilitation, throwing specialist Tom House helped him rebuild his throwing mechanics.
4. On his first visit to New Orleans, a wrong turn through hurricane-damaged Lakeview helped deepen his connection to the city after Katrina.
5. Sean Payton built the Saints offense collaboratively around Brees' strengths rather than forcing him into a pre-existing system.

Source summary: NBC Sports and the New Orleans Saints. Canonical source IDs: `identity-brees-nbc-recruiting`, `identity-brees-saints-making-champion`, `identity-brees-saints-lakeview`, `identity-brees-saints-hall-quotes`.

### Fran Tarkenton — QB

1. He grew up as a minister's son in a church-centered household and later recalled that he did not see his first movie until age 18.
2. In the expansion Vikings' first regular-season game, he came off the bench and threw four touchdown passes while running for another.
3. He said his scrambling style developed as self-preservation rather than as a designed quarterback identity.
4. His improvisational style clashed with coach Norm Van Brocklin's preference for traditional pocket quarterback play.
5. At Georgia, his fourth-down touchdown pass against Auburn in 1959 helped the Bulldogs secure the SEC championship.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame and National Football Foundation. Canonical source IDs: `identity-tarkenton-pro-hof`, `identity-tarkenton-hof-spotlight`, `identity-tarkenton-nff`.

### Kurt Warner — QB

1. He spent most of his Northern Iowa career as a reserve and had only one college season as the full-time starter before going undrafted.
2. After Green Bay cut him, he worked at a Hy-Vee grocery store for $5.50 an hour while trying to keep his football career alive.
3. His route to the NFL ran through the Arena Football League's Iowa Barnstormers and the Amsterdam Admirals of NFL Europe.
4. Trent Green's preseason injury unexpectedly opened the Rams' starting job for him in 1999.
5. After being displaced in Arizona and considering retirement, he reclaimed the starting job and led the Cardinals to the franchise's first Super Bowl appearance.

Source summary: Los Angeles Rams and Arizona Cardinals. Canonical source IDs: `identity-warner-rams-road`, `identity-warner-cardinals-renaissance`.

### Sammy Baugh — QB

1. He was a true three-phase star, playing quarterback, defensive back, and punter at a high level.
2. In one 1943 game against Detroit he threw four touchdown passes, intercepted four passes, and produced an 81-yard punt.
3. In TCU's 3-2 Sugar Bowl win he punted 14 times and averaged roughly 48 yards per punt.
4. After football he became closely associated with ranch life on a roughly 7,600-acre West Texas spread.
5. After Washington's 73-0 championship loss, he dryly joked that a dropped touchdown would only have made it 73-7.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame, National Football Foundation, and NFL.com / Associated Press. Canonical source IDs: `identity-baugh-pro-hof`, `identity-baugh-hof-1943`, `identity-baugh-nff`, `identity-baugh-nfl-ap`.

### Bart Starr — QB

1. A back injury and later benching complicated his Alabama career and left his NFL stock unusually uncertain.
2. Alabama basketball coach Johnny Dee helped his pro opportunity by tipping Packers scout Jack Vainisi about him.
3. After his rookie season he entered the Air Force but received a medical discharge because of his back condition.
4. Vince Lombardi's arrival in Green Bay in 1959 transformed a stalled early career and established Starr as the Packers' quarterback.
5. In the Ice Bowl, Starr proposed the quarterback sneak that became the game-winning touchdown.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-bart-starr`.

### Cam Newton — QB

1. He first visited Auburn as a high-school player in 2005, years before the transfer path that eventually brought him back.
2. His college route ran from Florida to Blinn College and then Auburn rather than directly to his defining school.
3. At Blinn he helped win a junior-college national championship, rebuilding his football path after Florida.
4. His December 2009 Auburn visit, proximity to his Atlanta roots, and the veteran roster helped make Auburn the final destination.
5. His entire Auburn playing career was the single 2010 season.

Source summary: Auburn Athletics. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-cam-newton`.

### Dan Marino — QB

1. A Pittsburgh native, he stayed home to play college football at Pitt.
2. Pitt coach Jackie Sherrill allowed him to preserve the naturally quick throwing motion that became central to his identity.
3. He lasted until Miami selected him 27th in the quarterback-heavy 1983 draft.
4. Don Shula shaped Miami's offense around Marino's release and aggressive downfield passing.
5. After an Achilles injury ended his 1993 season, he returned in the 1994 opener and immediately won a memorable shootout.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-dan-marino`.

### Josh Allen — QB

1. He grew up on his family's farm near Firebaugh, California.
2. His family contacted a large number of college programs trying to create a football opportunity, but he still began at Reedley College.
3. As a child he staged imaginary postgame interviews while dreaming about becoming a quarterback.
4. At Firebaugh he competed in football, basketball, and baseball.
5. Late physical growth contributed to his lack of major recruiting attention out of high school.

Source summary: Buffalo Bills. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-josh-allen`.

### Roger Staubach — QB

1. Before Navy, he spent a preparatory year at New Mexico Military Institute and competed in football, baseball, and basketball.
2. Dallas drafted him while his Navy service obligation meant his NFL career would be delayed for years.
3. He volunteered for duty in Vietnam and served as a supply officer.
4. His repeated late-game rallies produced the enduring nickname “Captain Comeback.”
5. His late playoff touchdown pass to Drew Pearson in 1975 helped popularize the football use of the phrase “Hail Mary.”

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-roger-staubach`.

### Adrian Peterson — RB

1. His father gave him the nickname “AD,” short for “All Day.”
2. He brought a serious sprint background to football from his high-school track career.
3. A collarbone injury created major uncertainty around him before Minnesota selected him in the 2007 draft.
4. Teammates and coaches repeatedly described an unusually intense approach even to practices and walkthroughs.
5. He returned for the 2012 season opener less than nine months after tearing his ACL and MCL.

Source summary: NFL.com. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-adrian-peterson`.

### Earl Campbell — RB

1. His East Texas roots produced the enduring “Tyler Rose” identity.
2. His mother, Ann, exercised unusually strong control over access to him during recruiting.
3. He spoke about football success partly as a way to improve his family's home, including fixing a leaking roof.
4. Even after becoming a college star, he continued doing summer construction work.
5. His 1978 Monday Night Football performance against Miami became an early defining moment of Houston's “Luv Ya Blue” era.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-earl-campbell`.

### Gale Sayers — RB

1. His speed and Kansas career produced the nickname “Kansas Comet.”
2. His relationship with Bears teammate Brian Piccolo grew from an interracial roommate pairing into a famous friendship.
3. Piccolo supported Sayers closely during rehabilitation from his 1968 knee injury.
4. When Sayers received the George S. Halas Courage Award, he publicly redirected the honor toward the seriously ill Piccolo.
5. His autobiography `I Am Third` and the Sayers-Piccolo friendship became the basis for `Brian's Song`.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-gale-sayers`.

### Marshall Faulk — RB

1. Major programs often projected him away from running back, while San Diego State gave him the chance to stay at the position he wanted.

3. The 1999 trade from Indianapolis to St. Louis placed him at the center of the “Greatest Show on Turf.”
4. His Rams arrival included a contract holdout before the breakthrough season began.
5. His value came from being a rare runner-receiver “chess piece” rather than a one-dimensional feature back.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-marshall-faulk`.

### Calvin Johnson — WR

2. As a child he carried the ironic nickname “Butterfingers.”
3. Lions teammate Roy Williams helped popularize the “Megatron” nickname.
4. He retired at age 30 after only nine NFL seasons.
5. He created the Calvin Johnson Jr. Foundation as a major part of his off-field identity.

### Alan Page — DL

1. As a young man he worked on a construction crew that helped build the Pro Football Hall of Fame, years before he was enshrined there.
2. He attended law school while still playing in the NFL.

### Joe Greene — DL

1. His “Mean Joe” nickname became linked to North Texas' “Mean Green” identity, although the school nickname was not literally created for him.
2. His upbringing included demanding outdoor and field work that helped shape his physical development.
3. A scouting description famously characterized him as “agile, mobile and hostile.”
4. The Coca-Cola commercial in which a young fan offers him a drink made him recognizable well beyond football.
5. His intimidating “Mean Joe” football persona contrasted sharply with accounts of his warmer off-field personality.

### Brian Urlacher — LB

1. New Mexico used him in a hybrid role known as the “Lobo Back.”
2. His college workload crossed defense, special teams, and offense.
3. New Mexico even used him as a red-zone receiver.
4. NFL evaluators debated whether his unusual athletic profile fit better at safety or linebacker.
5. Chicago initially tried him outside before moving him to middle linebacker, where his career took off.

### Charles Woodson — DB

1. In high school he was a standout running back as well as a defensive back.
2. Michigan used him on defense, offense, and returns rather than limiting him to one phase.
3. That rare two-way and return contribution was central to his Heisman-winning identity.
4. His famous punt-return touchdown celebration against Ohio State echoed Desmond Howard's earlier Michigan Heisman pose.
5. His apparent strip of Tom Brady in the 2001 postseason became the centerpiece of the “Tuck Rule” game.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-charles-woodson`.

### Ronnie Lott — DB

1. Growing up in an Air Force family meant moving frequently during childhood.
2. He practiced by creating imaginary game situations for himself as a child.
3. He played football, basketball, and baseball in high school.
4. After the 1985 season he chose to have the tip of his injured left pinky amputated rather than undergo a longer reconstruction.
5. He entered the NFL as a cornerback before becoming most closely identified with safety.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-ronnie-lott`.

### Andy Reid — Head coach

1. As a 13-year-old he looked so much larger than the other children during a nationally televised Punt, Pass & Kick appearance that the footage became a lasting curiosity.

3. BYU coach LaVell Edwards helped steer him toward coaching.

### Chuck Noll — Head coach

1. Before coaching he played in the NFL as a messenger guard, carrying play calls into the huddle.
2. He built a teacher-first coaching identity rather than a motivational-speaker persona.
3. Joe Greene later told a story of Noll discarding an opponent playbook because he wanted the Steelers to win through preparation rather than stolen information.
4. His reserved manner produced the nickname “The Emperor.”
5. His interests away from football included classical music, wine, aviation, and sailing.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-chuck-noll`.

### George Halas — Head coach

1. Before his football empire, he briefly played Major League Baseball as an outfielder for the New York Yankees.
2. His football organization began with the A.E. Staley company team in Decatur before moving to Chicago and becoming the Bears.
3. He participated in the meetings that founded the league that became the NFL.
4. His early football identity combined player, coach, owner, organizer, and league builder rather than just one role.
5. He became one of the key figures in bringing the T-formation into modern professional football.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-george-halas`.

### Jimmy Johnson — Head coach

1. At Arkansas he was a teammate of future Cowboys owner Jerry Jones on the 1964 national-championship team.
2. Jones hired him in 1989 to replace Tom Landry in Dallas.
3. The Herschel Walker trade used conditional draft-pick mechanics that became the engine of the Cowboys roster rebuild.
4. His Dallas identity centered on aggressive evaluation, draft acquisition, and rapid roster churn.
5. He and Jones split after back-to-back Super Bowl championships.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-jimmy-johnson`.

### Pete Carroll — Head coach

1. His Jets tenure is inseparable from the fake-spike collapse against Miami that helped define that short stint.
2. A later Buffalo coaching stop helped seed the “Win Forever” motivational framework he developed over time.
3. After leaving New England he studied leadership figures including John Wooden while rethinking his coaching philosophy.
4. “Always Compete” became a central phrase of his program-building identity.
5. Loud music, energy, and constant competition became trademarks of his practices.

Source summary: Seattle Seahawks. Canonical source ID: `identity-pr6-pete-carroll`.

### Jim Kelly — QB

1. Three games into his 1982 senior season at Miami, he completely separated his throwing shoulder and required reconstructive surgery.

### Joe Namath — QB

### John Elway — QB

### Bronko Nagurski — RB

1. Born Bronislau Nagurski to a Ukrainian immigrant family, he became known as Bronko while growing up around demanding farm work.
2. Minnesota used his rare versatility so broadly that in one 1929 game against Iowa he appeared at end, tackle, guard, halfback, and fullback.
3. A salary dispute contributed to him leaving the NFL after the 1937 season.

## Research exclusions and quality notes

The PR6 integration intentionally retained distinctive identity concepts rather than padding the count with duplicate résumé facts, generic awards/stat totals, repeated team/era phrasing, unsupported nickname lore, subjective “best ever” language, or sensational personal material that did not improve football identification.

Five current-main A-tier identities were deliberately not researched because the audit identified an obvious league-context mismatch before PR6 completion: Johnny Manziel, Tim Tebow, Vince Young, Nick Saban, and Urban Meyer. PR6 keeps that recognizability correction outside this identity-knowledge diff rather than creating a second ownership path.

## Architecture result

The existing PR4/PR5 model handled the 68-person PR6 slice without a schema fork. Every retained fact remains presentation-neutral, keyed to the canonical football subject ID, tagged as `distinctive-identity`, verified, and linked to the existing `FootballFactSource` provenance shape.

No Who Am I-only trivia database, second football registry, alternate query path, runtime web lookup, runtime sports API dependency, runtime LLM truth judgment, clue generator, tier owner, or launch roster was added. The reusable football person-identity module remains the sole owner for this research.
