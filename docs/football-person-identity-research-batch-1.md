# Football person identity research — NFL A-tier batch 1

This is the human-reviewable continuation of `docs/football-person-identity-research-pilot.md` for Who Am I Rebuild PR5. PR4 proved the shared knowledge/provenance architecture on 12 NFL A-tier identities. PR5 keeps that architecture unchanged and adds a second, larger research batch selected only from the existing canonical NFL A-tier launch pool.

Research rule: retain memorable person-level identity concepts, not another copy of structural resume facts. Every retained concept is represented in `src/features/back-room/footballPersonIdentityKnowledge.ts` with source provenance. Rejected items are recorded here so weak, contradictory, generic, or sensational material is not silently converted into clue truth.

## PR5 identities

### Tom Brady — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- At Junipero Serra High School he played quarterback and catcher, and the Montreal Expos selected him in the 18th round of the 1995 MLB Draft.
- He redshirted his first season at Michigan in 1995.
- During Michigan's 1997 national-title season he was still Brian Griese's backup.
- As a senior in 1999 he shared early-game quarterback time with highly recruited sophomore Drew Henson before repeatedly finishing games.
- He closed his Michigan career by throwing for 369 yards and four touchdowns in an overtime Orange Bowl win over Alabama.

Source summary: New England Patriots draft biography and Patriots player biography, both covering his Serra baseball background, Michigan path, and final college season.

Rejected: generic “nobody believed in him” framing and NFL trophy/count material. The documented baseball/Michigan path is more distinctive and avoids turning a later draft result into a vague mythology.

### Peyton Manning — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He arrived at Tennessee carrying a direct football-family identity as Archie Manning's son and later became part of the first father-son player pairing in the College Football Hall of Fame.
- He was an Academic All-America selection at Tennessee.
- He accumulated enough credits to graduate by the end of his junior year.
- He passed on entering the NFL Draft after the 1996 season and returned to Tennessee for his senior year.
- That return ended with Tennessee winning the 1997 SEC championship.

Source summary: Tennessee Athletics Academic All-America Hall of Fame feature and Tennessee's College Football Hall of Fame coverage.

Rejected: generic Manning-family fame, NFL passing totals, and award counts already represented by resume knowledge. The retained family fact is specifically the first father-son College Football Hall of Fame player distinction.

### Aaron Rodgers — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- His major-college path began with one season at Butte College before he transferred to California.
- Butte went 10-1, won its conference, finished No. 2 nationally among junior colleges, and Rodgers earned conference/region MVP recognition.
- At Cal he did not take over the starting job until the fifth game of his first season, then went 7-3 as the starter.
- In the 2003 Big Game against Stanford he generated 414 yards of total offense, one of the performances that accelerated his national profile.
- At the 2005 NFL Draft he waited for hours in the green room before Green Bay selected him 24th overall.

Source summary: California Athletics career biography for the Butte/Cal path and NFL reporting on his 2005 draft-day fall.

Rejected: the absolute claim that he had “no Division I offers.” The sourced junior-college path is strong enough without overstating the recruiting record.

### Drew Brees — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- Football was only one of his serious sports growing up; he also played baseball and basketball and once viewed baseball as a realistic college path.
- At Westlake High School he went 28-0-1 as the starting quarterback over his final two seasons and won a state championship as a senior.
- One of his high-school games included 83 pass attempts, an unusually early example of the spread-style volume passing that later became common in Texas.
- Despite that production, the major Texas programs did not offer him a football scholarship, helping send him to Purdue.
- Purdue materials recorded the nicknames “Cool Brees” and “Hurricane Drew” during his college career.

Source summary: NFL feature on Texas quarterback development and Purdue Athletics' Brees biography.

Rejected: “Purdue was his only offer.” Sources support that he was lightly recruited and lacked offers from the major Texas programs, but the stronger absolute version was not necessary.

### Joe Montana — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He arrived at Notre Dame as a skinny, homesick freshman in a seven-quarterback class and fell as low as seventh on the depth chart.
- Early uncertainty and homesickness were serious enough that leaving Notre Dame was part of his freshman adjustment story.
- His “Comeback Kid” identity began taking shape with fourth-quarter comeback wins over Purdue and Clemson in 1977.
- He then helped Notre Dame upset No. 1 Texas in the Cotton Bowl to secure the 1977 national championship.
- In the 1979 Cotton Bowl “Chicken Soup Game,” he returned after battling illness and hypothermia to lead a 22-point fourth-quarter comeback over Houston.

Source summary: Notre Dame Athletics' Strong of Heart profile and official Notre Dame retrospectives on the Comeback Kid and Cotton Bowl games.

Rejected: splitting the Chicken Soup Game into several derivative “facts.” The illness, return, and comeback are one memorable concept.

### Dan Marino — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He grew up in Pittsburgh's Oakland neighborhood in the immediate shadow of the University of Pittsburgh and stayed home to play for Pitt.
- At Central Catholic he was a high-level baseball player as well as a quarterback, going 23-0 as a pitcher and hitting above .500 as a senior.
- The Kansas City Royals selected him in the fourth round of the 1979 MLB Draft.
- As a Pitt true freshman he moved into the lineup after an injury to the starter and helped lead the Panthers to the Fiesta Bowl.
- His final-play profile at Pitt included a late winning touchdown pass to John Brown in the 1982 Sugar Bowl, one of his signature college moments.

Source summary: Pitt Athletics' College Football Hall of Fame coverage plus MLB's multisport-draft retrospective.

Rejected: rumors and speculation about why he fell in the 1983 NFL Draft. Those stories are sensational, disputed, and unnecessary for recognizing Marino.

### John Elway — QB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- The Kansas City Royals drafted him out of high school in the 18th round in 1979.
- The New York Yankees then selected him in the second round of the 1981 MLB Draft while he was at Stanford.
- In 42 games for Class A Oneonta he hit .318 with four home runs and 13 stolen bases.
- He returned to Stanford football rather than continue immediately up the Yankees' minor-league ladder.
- His legitimate baseball option gave him unusual leverage to refuse to play for Baltimore after the Colts drafted him first overall, leading to the trade to Denver.

Source summary: MLB/Yankees draft retrospectives on Elway's baseball career and the role baseball played in his NFL draft decision.

Rejected: speculation that he “would have been a major-league star.” The actual draft position and minor-league performance are enough.

### Emmitt Smith — RB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- At Escambia High School he became a national prep player of the year and one of the most productive high-school runners of his era.
- His prep career produced 8,804 rushing yards and 106 touchdowns, with 100-yard rushing games in 45 of 49 appearances.
- Even after that production, Florida's own retrospective notes that he was widely labeled “too small” or “too slow.”
- At Florida he reached 1,000 rushing yards by his seventh game, earlier than any college freshman had done at the time.
- After his junior year he became the first Florida player to declare early for the NFL Draft when the league opened the traditional draft to underclassmen in 1990.

Source summary: Florida Athletics' Gator Greats profile, 1987 media guide/history, rushing-record retrospective, and underclassman draft history.

Rejected: generic career rushing-record material. The high-school scale, skepticism, immediate freshman arrival, and first-Florida early-entry decision are more personal and distinctive.

### Adrian Peterson — RB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- His “A.D.” nickname comes from his father calling him “All Day” because of his childhood energy.
- He was a serious multi-sport high-school athlete who also competed in track and basketball.
- He publicly committed to Oklahoma around the U.S. Army All-American game after scoring twice in the showcase.
- He did not start Oklahoma's first three games as a freshman; an injury ahead of him helped open the featured role.
- That freshman season included an NCAA freshman record of nine consecutive 100-yard rushing games.

Source summary: Oklahoma Athletics recruiting, freshman-season, and award coverage.

Rejected: family tragedy and other deeply personal material that may be biographically important but is not needed for a recognition game.

### LaDainian Tomlinson — RB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- The Waco native stayed close to home for college at TCU and became the centerpiece of the program's late-1990s resurgence.
- Against UTEP in 1999 he rushed for 406 yards and six touchdowns in one game.
- He became the first TCU player to post a 2,000-yard rushing season.
- His off-field routine with the Chargers included purchasing 21 tickets to each home game for children through his community program.
- At retirement he signed a one-day contract so he could retire as a Charger, while TCU's No. 5 remained one of the central symbols of his college legacy.

Source summary: TCU Athletics retrospectives on his career, UTEP game, community work, and retirement.

Rejected: duplicating rushing-title and pro-stat totals that already belong in the structured resume layer.

### Marshall Faulk — RB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- San Diego State opened his freshman season with him third on the running-back depth chart after two early fumbles.
- In the second game of his college career he erupted for 386 rushing yards and seven touchdowns in only three quarters against Pacific.
- He became the first freshman to lead the nation in both rushing and scoring.
- He also became the first freshman running back named an Associated Press first-team All-American.
- San Diego State later retired his No. 28 and inducted him into its athletics hall of fame in his first year of eligibility.

Source summary: San Diego State Athletics history and Hall of Fame retrospectives.

Rejected: padding with NFL award totals. His almost-immediate shift from third-string freshman to national phenomenon is the distinctive story.

### Jim Brown — RB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- At Manhasset High School he competed in five sports and was a dominant basketball player as well as a football star.
- At Syracuse he lettered in four sports: football, lacrosse, basketball, and track.
- His lacrosse career was elite enough that he is remembered as one of that sport's all-time greats as well as a football legend.
- He participated in Army ROTC at Syracuse, was commissioned as a second lieutenant, and later served in the Army Reserve.
- In 1988 he founded the Amer-I-Can program, using a life-skills curriculum aimed at helping people in disadvantaged and high-risk communities.

Source summary: Syracuse Athletics' institutional remembrance and historical biography.

Rejected: movie credits and generic NFL dominance statistics. The multisport, military, and community dimensions are more useful person identity.

### Randy Moss — WR — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He was an elite high-school basketball player as well as a football star, earning West Virginia state player-of-the-year recognition in both sports.
- At Marshall he also competed in track and won Southern Conference sprint titles in the 60 and 100 meters.
- His first Marshall football team went 15-0 and won the Division I-AA national championship.
- That 1996 season included 29 receiving touchdowns, making his arrival at Marshall immediate and unmistakable.
- In 1997 he won the Biletnikoff and Paul Warfield awards and was a Heisman finalist before entering the NFL.

Source summary: Marshall Athletics Hall of Fame and institutional Moss retrospectives.

Rejected: legal/recruiting controversies attached to his earlier Notre Dame and Florida State paths. They are sensational, not necessary, and less useful than his verified multisport identity.

### Calvin Johnson — WR — 5 concepts

Facts:
- On his first day in Georgia Tech's program he posted a 42-inch vertical jump, then a program record.
- As a young high-school receiver he was once nicknamed “Butterfingers.”
- He responded by repeatedly drilling his hands until they blistered, turning an early weakness into one of his defining strengths.
- He grew roughly five inches between his freshman and sophomore years of high school, changing his athletic profile dramatically.
- Baseball had been his longest-running sport; Georgia Tech's baseball staff was interested in him, but he chose not to add college baseball to football.

Source summary: Georgia Tech Athletics Hall of Fame profile and official Johnson biography.

Rejected: the generic “Megatron” nickname as a standalone fact because the retained early “Butterfingers” story is more surprising and person-specific unless a strong nickname-origin source is needed later.

### Terrell Owens — WR — 5 concepts

Facts:
- At Chattanooga he was a three-sport college athlete in football, men's basketball, and track and field.
- He earned two basketball letters and played on teams that won Southern Conference regular-season and tournament championships and reached the NCAA tournament.
- His football development at Chattanooga included a 20-game reception streak across the 1994 and 1995 seasons.
- San Francisco found him at the smaller program and selected him in the third round of the 1996 NFL Draft.
- In Super Bowl XXXIX he returned only seven weeks after suffering a broken leg and torn ankle ligaments and caught nine passes for 122 yards.

Source summary: Chattanooga Athletics' SoCon Hall of Fame and UTC Hall of Fame coverage plus Pro Football Hall of Fame draft history.

Rejected: feud, celebration, and personality gossip. The multisport college path and Super Bowl injury return are stronger recognition material and much easier to source cleanly.

### Larry Fitzgerald — WR — 5 concepts

Facts:
- As a teenager he worked as a Minnesota Vikings ball boy, giving him an unusually close view of NFL preparation before college.
- That job let him study and build relationships with receivers including Cris Carter and Randy Moss.
- Academic trouble sent him to Valley Forge Military Academy for a year before he reached Pitt.
- His mother's death from breast cancer while he was young shaped long-running breast-cancer awareness and support work later in his life.
- His off-field identity includes extensive international service work, including hearing-aid missions and water/agriculture projects abroad.

Source summary: NFL feature on his offseason preparation, Pitt Athletics' A Football Life material, and Pitt biography/community coverage.

Rejected: using a precise “number of countries visited” as a clue concept. It changes over time and adds less recognition value than the underlying global-service identity.

### John Mackey — TE — 5 concepts

Facts:
- Syracuse used him as a running back before his pro career became synonymous with tight end.
- When Baltimore drafted him, the Colts initially considered him as a fullback before settling him at tight end.
- In 1966 he scored six touchdowns of at least 50 yards, an unusual big-play profile for the position in that era.
- In Super Bowl V a deflected pass became a 75-yard Mackey touchdown, one of the game's signature plays.
- His later dementia struggle helped inspire the NFL/NFLPA “88 Plan,” named for his jersey number, to assist former players with dementia-related care.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame Mackey biography/retrospectives and Syracuse Athletics remembrance.

Rejected: the overstatement that he “single-handedly invented the modern tight end.” Hall sources support that he helped redefine the position, not that football changed because of one person alone.

### Anthony Muñoz — OL — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He was a high-school football, basketball, and baseball athlete before USC.
- At USC he was good enough in baseball to pitch for the Trojans' 1978 national championship team.
- His physical growth increasingly pushed his athletic future from baseball toward football.
- A major knee injury cost him almost all of his final USC football season, but he returned for the Rose Bowl.
- Cincinnati still selected him third overall despite the knee concerns, a draft gamble that became central to his career story.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame profile, College Football Hall of Fame Hispanic Heritage feature, and Muñoz Foundation biography.

Rejected: using surgery-count trivia when sources describe the knee history with different levels of detail. The injury-and-Rose-Bowl return is the cleaner concept.

### Joe Thomas — OL — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He was a three-sport high-school athlete in football, basketball, and track.
- His prep football identity was two-way: he earned major recognition at both offensive tackle and defensive end.
- Wisconsin initially used him as a blocking tight end, and he even started at defensive end in a bowl game before becoming an elite offensive tackle.
- He was also a nationally competitive shot-putter and set a Wisconsin indoor shot-put record.
- Rather than attend the 2007 NFL Draft in New York, he went fishing on Lake Michigan with his father and took Cleveland's No. 3 pick phone call from the boat.

Source summary: Wisconsin Athletics biography/history and NFL coverage of his 2007 draft-day fishing trip.

Rejected: generic durability and Pro Bowl totals. The position changes, track background, and fishing-day choice distinguish the person much better.

### Reggie White — DL — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He became an ordained minister as a teenager, well before his NFL career.
- His faith and football identity combined in the nickname “Minister of Defense.”
- A Chattanooga-area high-school star, he did not receive major statewide publicity until relatively late in his senior season before his recruitment exploded.
- At Tennessee he produced a four-sack game against The Citadel, then a school record.
- Before joining Philadelphia he played two professional seasons for the Memphis Showboats of the USFL.

Source summary: Tennessee Athletics biographies and College Football Hall of Fame coverage.

Rejected: generic sack totals and award counts. The ordained-minister identity and USFL path are substantially more distinctive.

### Bruce Smith — DL — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He did not begin playing organized football until his sophomore year of high school.
- By his senior year he had developed quickly enough to become an all-state player.
- At Virginia Tech his unusual speed for a defensive lineman became a defining part of how coaches and teammates described his game.
- Early in college he weighed more than 300 pounds, then transformed his conditioning and playing weight dramatically.
- His later training became famous for relentless conditioning work, including heavy StairMaster sessions.

Source summary: Virginia Tech Athletics' all-time All-America feature and Smith's Pro Football Hall of Fame enshrinement material.

Rejected: presenting exact weight-change numbers as if they were a formal record. The source is recollection; the verified concept is the major conditioning transformation.

### Joe Greene — DL — 5 concepts

Facts:
- North Texas' “Mean Green” team nickname was not named for Joe Greene; it began as a cheer for the defense.
- After Pittsburgh drafted him, local pro-football media adapted his college association with the Mean Green into the individual “Mean Joe Greene” nickname.
- In 1969 he became the first North Texas player ever selected in the first round of the NFL Draft.
- He initially was disappointed that the struggling Steelers had drafted him, then became the central figure in the franchise's culture change.
- His 1979 Coca-Cola commercial, in which he tossed his jersey to a young fan, became an iconic piece of his public identity and won major advertising recognition.

Source summary: North Texas Athletics' official Mean Green origin history/Hall of Fame page plus Pittsburgh Steelers history of Greene and the Coca-Cola commercial.

Rejected: the commonly repeated false claim that North Texas adopted “Mean Green” because of Greene. The university explicitly says the link is a coincidence; the individual “Mean Joe” label came later.

### Brian Urlacher — LB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- At Lovington High School in New Mexico he helped lead a 14-0 state championship team.
- New Mexico built a hybrid “Lobo Back” role around him rather than using him as a conventional linebacker.
- As a senior he contributed on defense, at wide receiver, and as a punt returner.
- That season he led New Mexico in tackles while also leading the team in touchdowns and punt-return average.
- Lovington later named its football field for him, and he helped fund a major indoor facility at the school.

Source summary: University of New Mexico and New Mexico Activities Association retrospectives.

Rejected: flattening his college identity to “played linebacker.” The hybrid role and three-way senior usage are exactly what make his path distinctive.

### Dick Butkus — LB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- A Chicago native, he stayed in-state to become one of the defining players in Illinois football history.
- Illinois used him as a two-way player at linebacker and center.
- He was a centerpiece of the 1963 Big Ten championship team that won the Rose Bowl.
- In 1964 a defensive player finished third in the Heisman Trophy voting with Butkus in that spot.
- Illinois retired his No. 50, and the annual Butkus Award later made his name the national shorthand for elite linebacker play.

Source summary: Illinois Athletics remembrance and retired-number history.

Rejected: unsourced superlatives such as “most feared player ever.” The two-way role, Heisman placement, and award legacy are stronger verifiable identity facts.

### Troy Polamalu — DB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- He grew up rooting for Oregon State but ultimately chose USC.
- In high school he earned all-state recognition on both sides of the ball as a running back and safety.
- USC used him as more than a conventional safety; his college impact also included blocked kicks and major special-teams plays.
- His long, flowing hair became one of the most immediately recognizable visual identities in football.
- That hair identity crossed into mainstream advertising, including national shampoo commercials.

Source summary: USC Athletics profile, All-America coverage, and USC Hall of Fame biography.

Rejected: folklore about hair-insurance values and unsourced explanations for why he wore it long. The visual fact and verified advertising connection are enough.

### Ed Reed — DB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- Miami moved him from strong safety to free safety as a senior specifically to let him roam and affect more of the field.
- He played through a separated shoulder and injured ribs during his college career without missing the defining stretch of the season.
- Against Boston College in 2001 he took a teammate's interception via lateral and ran it 80 yards for the touchdown that sealed the game.
- His college impact extended to blocked punts and field goals, not only interceptions.
- Miami described him as the heart-and-soul leader of the 2001 national championship team.

Source summary: University of Miami official player biography.

Rejected: repeating generic interception totals already available in structural statistics. The role change, lateral, toughness, special teams, and leadership are the person-level layer.

### Charles Woodson — DB — 5 concepts

Facts:
- Before becoming a defensive icon he was Ohio's Mr. Football as a high-school running back.
- Michigan was the school he had wanted to attend, and he arrived with an immediate opportunity to play.
- He entered Michigan's starting lineup by the second game of his freshman season.
- Lloyd Carr later asked him to add an offensive role, leading to regular snaps as a receiver while he remained an elite cornerback.
- That legitimate offense-defense-special-teams identity culminated in him becoming the rare two-way player to win the Heisman Trophy in the modern two-platoon era.

Source summary: Heisman Trust Woodson biography and Pro Football Hall of Fame profile.

Rejected: exact high-school rushing yardage because two otherwise strong sources conflict (2,028 versus 2,208). The fact that he won Ohio Mr. Football as a running back is retained without the disputed number.

### Andy Reid — head coach — 5 concepts

Facts:
- In high school he was an all-league baseball pitcher as well as a football lineman.
- His college path started at Glendale Junior College rather than at BYU.
- At Glendale he earned all-conference recognition and a team “most inspirational” honor before transferring.
- At BYU he played offensive tackle and had a medical-redshirt season.
- His coaching career began at BYU as a graduate assistant under LaVell Edwards after completing his playing career and graduate education.

Source summary: BYU Athletics' official Andy Reid player biography.

Rejected: the viral Punt, Pass & Kick clip as a retained research fact. It is memorable, but this batch already has five stronger concepts from a single authoritative primary source and did not need internet folklore to fill the count.

### John Madden — head coach — 5 concepts

Facts:
- At Cal Poly he played on both the offensive and defensive lines and also played baseball.
- Philadelphia selected him in the 21st round of the 1958 NFL Draft.
- A knee injury in training camp ended his playing career before it could become an NFL career.
- During rehabilitation, quarterback Norm Van Brocklin pulled him into intensive film study, which Madden later described as one of the great educations of his football life.
- He earned bachelor's and master's degrees in education, a background that fit the teacher identity he carried into coaching.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame career retrospective and Cal Poly remembrance.

Rejected: broadcaster and video-game accomplishments for this batch. They are highly recognizable but belong to a later media-identity expansion if needed; the playing-injury-film-study-to-teacher path is less generic.

### Don Shula — head coach — 5 concepts

Facts:
- His parents initially opposed football strongly enough that as a teenager he forged their signatures to get permission to play.
- After John Carroll he spent a year coaching high-school football before the Cleveland Browns drafted him as a player.
- His early pro career was interrupted by Ohio National Guard service during the Korean War era.
- Before becoming a coach he spent seven NFL seasons as a defensive back.
- Baltimore hired him as its head coach at age 33, making him the youngest head coach in the NFL at the time.

Source summary: Pro Football Hall of Fame Shula remembrance, Cleveland Browns history, and Miami Dolphins career timeline.

Rejected: career-win totals, undefeated-season facts, and championship counts. Those are structural resume facts; the forged signature, military service, playing path, and unusually young head-coach appointment add the second identity dimension PR5 is meant to build.
