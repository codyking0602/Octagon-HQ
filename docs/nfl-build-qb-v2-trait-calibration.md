# NFL Build a QB v2 — trait definitions and calibration anchors

## Purpose

This document defines the grading constitution for NFL Build a QB v2 before the 112-QB population is graded.

The order is intentional:

1. Define exactly what each trait measures.
2. Lock historical calibration anchors.
3. Grade the full population relative to those anchors.
4. Run pairwise contradiction audits.
5. Inspect the natural distribution only after grading.

The generator may later select from these grades, but it may never manufacture, raise, lower, or infer a grade to improve game shape.

## Global grading semantic

NFL Build a QB grades the quarterback's **best established NFL version of the trait**.

This is not:
- career greatness,
- career average,
- awards,
- team success,
- a one-play maximum,
- prospect projection,
- reputation.

A trait peak must be demonstrated enough to represent a real NFL ability, not one isolated throw or short novelty sample. Injuries, aging, and late-career decline do not erase an established prime trait. For active or very young quarterbacks, grade only what has actually been demonstrated in the NFL to date.

Each trait stands alone. A lower-overall quarterback may own an elite trait, and an all-time-great quarterback may have a merely good or average trait.

## Grade scale

Use 0.5-point increments.

The scale is descriptive, not quota-based:

- **99.0–100.0 — historical ceiling:** legitimate best-ever territory for the trait.
- **96.0–98.5 — historic / rare elite:** one of the defining NFL examples of the trait.
- **92.0–95.5 — elite:** clearly premium, game-changing trait.
- **87.0–91.5 — high-end:** major strength; well above normal starter level.
- **82.0–86.5 — above average:** clear positive trait.
- **76.0–81.5 — functional / solid:** NFL-starter-caliber tool without being a major advantage.
- **69.0–75.5 — below average:** meaningful limitation, but usable.
- **60.0–68.5 — major limitation:** regularly constrains the quarterback.
- **Below 60 — severe limitation:** use only when the NFL evidence genuinely warrants it.

These bands do not need equal populations. One trait may naturally have more 90s or more 70s than another.

# Arm

## Definition

**Arm measures functional NFL throwing talent: the quarterback's ability to generate useful velocity, distance, and release solutions across NFL throws.**

Evaluate:

- velocity on drive throws, especially outside the numbers and into tight windows;
- deep-distance capacity without requiring a perfect launch platform;
- ability to attack every field level;
- arm elasticity / torque from varied bases and arm angles;
- ability to retain useful velocity when the platform is imperfect;
- difficult opposite-hash, boundary, and late-window throws that demand physical arm access.

Do **not** grade Arm as maximum throwing distance alone. Touch, trajectory finesse, and placement quality belong primarily to Accuracy; Arm only owns the physical ability to generate the throw.

Do not reward:
- whether the quarterback chose the correct target — Processing;
- whether the ball arrived at the ideal location — Accuracy;
- the ability to escape or create the moving platform — Mobility.

A quarterback can have a huge arm and poor Accuracy or Processing. A quarterback can also be highly mobile without possessing elite Arm.

## Arm calibration anchors

- **Josh Allen — 100.0:** historical ceiling for functional NFL arm power: rare drive velocity and distance that remain available from imperfect platforms.
- **Patrick Mahomes — 99.5:** just below the pure-power ceiling, with exceptional velocity retention, arm-angle versatility, elasticity, and off-platform access.
- **John Elway — 99.0:** historical power/velocity benchmark.
- **Anthony Richardson — 98.5:** rare raw NFL power and velocity; the grade is for the demonstrated physical arm, not overall quarterback performance.
- **Brett Favre — 98.0:** historical fastball/power benchmark.
- **Justin Herbert — 98.0:** rare modern power and full-field drive capacity.
- **Matthew Stafford — 98.0:** rare drive velocity, deep power, and angle/platform flexibility.
- **Aaron Rodgers — 97.5:** elite velocity plus unusually efficient generation from varied platforms.
- **Dan Marino — 97.5:** elite drive velocity and release power across demanding NFL throws.
- **Michael Vick — 97.5:** exceptional velocity and deep power from a compact release.
- **Jay Cutler — 95.0:** specialist anchor: lower overall quarterback value does not prevent a truly elite Arm grade.
- **Joe Flacco — 94.0:** elite deep/drive arm without the same total functional package as the ceiling group.
- **Joe Burrow — 90.5:** high-end functional arm, but physical throwing power is not the defining trait.
- **Drew Brees — 80.0:** functional NFL arm whose elite value came much more from placement and processing than physical power.

# Accuracy

## Definition

**Accuracy measures repeatable ball placement: how consistently the quarterback puts the football where the intended receiver can best complete the play.**

Evaluate:

- short, intermediate, and deep placement;
- leading receivers away from leverage and contact;
- back-shoulder, boundary, layered, and touch placement;
- catchability and location consistency;
- precision under pressure and from imperfect platforms;
- ability to reproduce placement rather than merely flash it.

Completion percentage is evidence, not the grade. Scheme-created throws, checkdowns, receiver separation, and yards after catch cannot mechanically create an elite Accuracy grade.

Do not reward:
- identifying the correct coverage or target — Processing;
- raw velocity / distance — Arm;
- escaping to create the throw — Mobility.

If a quarterback throws accurately while moving, the **movement creation** belongs to Mobility and the **ball placement after movement** belongs to Accuracy.

## Accuracy calibration anchors

- **Drew Brees — 100.0:** historical placement/consistency ceiling across field levels.
- **Joe Burrow — 99.5:** modern historical-ceiling placement benchmark.
- **Aaron Rodgers — 99.0:** rare placement precision combined with low-error consistency across difficult throws.
- **Peyton Manning — 98.5:** elite repeatable placement with exceptional timing/location control.
- **Tom Brady — 98.5:** elite placement, especially in timing, leverage, and pressure contexts.
- **Jayden Daniels — 97.5:** demonstrated elite early-career NFL ball location; youth does not add a bonus beyond what has been shown.
- **Joe Montana — 98.0:** historical precision/touch benchmark.
- **Patrick Mahomes — 96.0:** elite placement across conventional and unconventional platforms.
- **Troy Aikman — 96.0:** historical high-end timing and placement benchmark.
- **Tua Tagovailoa — 96.0:** elite anticipatory placement; scheme efficiency alone does not own the grade.
- **Josh Allen — 93.5:** developed into elite placement at peak, clearly below his Arm ceiling.
- **Lamar Jackson — 93.5:** peak version now has genuinely elite ball placement; this grade does not come from rushing value.
- **Cam Newton — 68.5:** major physical tools and playmaking value do not erase inconsistent repeatable placement.

# Processing

## Definition

**Processing measures how quickly and correctly the quarterback understands the defense and converts that information into the right football decision.**

Evaluate:

- pre-snap identification and protection awareness;
- post-snap coverage recognition;
- progression speed and discipline;
- timing and anticipation;
- leverage recognition;
- decision quality under pressure;
- manipulation of defenders with eyes, timing, and sequencing;
- avoidance of unnecessary negative decisions.

Processing is not an IQ label and should never be inferred from personality, interviews, reputation, or style.

Do not reward:
- the physical ability to make the throw — Arm;
- the precise location of the throw — Accuracy;
- physically escaping a failed play — Mobility.

A quarterback can compensate for weaker Arm or Mobility with elite Processing, and a physically gifted quarterback can grade much lower here if reads/decisions consistently limit him.

## Processing calibration anchors

- **Peyton Manning — 100.0:** historical benchmark for pre-snap command, recognition, sequencing, and control.
- **Tom Brady — 100.0:** historical benchmark for recognition, progression, situational decision quality, and pressure answers.
- **Aaron Rodgers — 99.0:** elite recognition and decision control with rare mistake avoidance at peak.
- **Drew Brees — 99.0:** elite diagnostic speed and progression/timing command.
- **Joe Montana — 99.0:** historical benchmark for timing, progression discipline, and high-leverage decision quality.
- **Joe Burrow — 98.5:** elite modern timing, recognition, and progression benchmark.
- **Patrick Mahomes — 98.0:** elite processing plus the ability to preserve good decisions as structure breaks down.
- **Lamar Jackson — 95.5:** peak version reflects elite recognition and decision growth independent of his rushing value.
- **Josh Allen — 95.5:** peak version reflects elite decision growth independent of his arm and rushing traits.
- **Matthew Stafford — 94.5:** high-end coverage recognition and difficult-window decision capability.
- **Alex Smith — 91.5:** strong decision-discipline anchor without implying elite physical traits.
- **Jared Goff — 93.0:** high-end in-structure processing with more vulnerability when the picture changes under pressure.
- **Michael Vick — 75.0:** extraordinary physical tools do not automatically create a high Processing grade.
- **Johnny Manziel — 52.0:** severe NFL structure/decision limitations despite real improvisational talent.

# Mobility

## Definition

**Mobility measures functional offensive value created by quarterback movement. It includes rushing threat, escape/outside-structure creation, and movement within the pocket.**

Three lenses matter:

1. **Rushing threat** — designed-run value, scrambling danger, speed, elusiveness, power where relevant.
2. **Escape / outside-structure creation** — avoiding pressure, extending plays, changing launch points, and preserving pass options after structure breaks.
3. **Pocket movement** — climbing, sliding, resetting, subtle evasion, and maintaining a usable throwing platform.

Rushing production is substantial evidence, but Mobility is **not** a rushing-yards leaderboard.

There is no rigid mathematical weighting. As a calibration lens, rushing threat is the largest component, outside-structure creation is close behind, and pocket movement is meaningful enough to prevent a great pocket mover from grading like a statue.

Do not reward:
- the arm talent used after escaping — Arm;
- the placement of the moving throw — Accuracy;
- the mental decision about where to go with the ball — Processing.

## Mobility calibration anchors

- **Lamar Jackson — 100.0:** historical ceiling because elite rushing threat, escape ability, and creation all coexist.
- **Michael Vick — 99.5:** historical open-field / escape benchmark with extraordinary movement stress on a defense.
- **Robert Griffin III — 99.0:** elite peak rushing threat and explosive movement before injuries altered the profile.
- **Anthony Richardson — 98.5:** rare physical rushing/escape ceiling demonstrated in the NFL, with availability not counted as a trait penalty.
- **Kyler Murray — 98.5:** elite rushing/escape threat with exceptional short-area movement.
- **Josh Allen — 98.0:** elite functional movement through power, scrambling, designed-run value, and creation.
- **Steve Young — 96.5:** historical high-end combination of rushing, escape, and functional quarterback movement.
- **Patrick Mahomes — 95.5:** extraordinary escape, pocket manipulation, and outside-structure creation without Lamar-level designed-run threat.
- **Aaron Rodgers — 92.5:** elite prime pocket movement, rollout/escape ability, and functional scrambling without being an elite volume runner.
- **Tony Romo — 85.5:** strong pocket/escape mobility despite not being a major designed-run weapon.
- **Joe Burrow — 83.0:** useful pocket movement and scrambling, but movement is not a defining offensive weapon.
- **Tom Brady — 72.0:** almost no rushing threat, but excellent subtle pocket movement keeps him well above the true statue floor.
- **Philip Rivers — 58.0:** severe movement limitation; anticipation and processing often compensated for it.

# Independence / contradiction rules

During full-population grading:

- Never derive a trait from overall career rank.
- Never force four traits to average to a preconceived overall.
- Never lower an elite specialist because the quarterback was mediocre overall.
- Never raise a weak trait because the quarterback was an all-time great.
- Never use awards, rings, wins, or fame as trait evidence by themselves.
- Never force a grade distribution or target count by band.
- Use the anchors as comparative commitments. If a new grade implies an ordering we do not believe, resolve the contradiction before locking the grade.

Examples:

- If Jay Cutler's Arm is 95, any quarterback graded above him must genuinely have a stronger functional arm case.
- If Tom Brady's Mobility is 72, slower runners with materially worse pocket movement should not automatically grade above him just because they accumulated more incidental rushing yards.
- If Cam Newton's Accuracy is 68.5, a quarterback placed below him must have a genuinely weaker repeatable NFL placement profile.
- If Alex Smith's Processing is 91.5, a materially higher grade should reflect clearly stronger read/decision capability, not merely a more successful career.
